# Logical Fallacies Catalog — 30+ Fallacies for Research Review

## Purpose
Reference catalog of logical fallacies commonly encountered in research and art-research argumentation. Used by the devils_advocate_agent. Examples are drawn from practice-based art research; the fallacy structures are genre-neutral.

## Formal Fallacies (Invalid Logical Structure)

### 1. Affirming the Consequent
**Structure**: If P then Q; Q is true; therefore P is true.
**Example**: "If a work is generative, audiences find it surprising. This work surprised audiences. Therefore, it must be generative."
**Problem**: Q can have multiple causes.

### 2. Denying the Antecedent
**Structure**: If P then Q; not P; therefore not Q.
**Example**: "If a work is interactive, it engages the audience. This work isn't interactive. Therefore, it doesn't engage the audience."
**Problem**: Engagement can come from other qualities (image, sound, scale).

### 3. Undistributed Middle
**Structure**: All A are B; All C are B; therefore All A are C.
**Example**: "All acclaimed media artworks use real-time computation. Our work uses real-time computation. Therefore, our work is acclaimed."
**Problem**: B (real-time computation) is shared but doesn't link A and C.

### 4. False Dilemma / False Dichotomy
**Structure**: Either A or B; not A; therefore B.
**Example**: "Either the work is conceptual or it is purely aesthetic."
**Problem**: Many hybrid positions exist.

## Informal Fallacies

### Relevance Fallacies

### 5. Ad Hominem
**Description**: Attacking the person rather than the argument.
**Research Example**: "This artist's claims about the work are unreliable because they are self-taught."
**Correct Approach**: Evaluate the work and the evidence, not the author's background (though COI should be noted).

### 6. Appeal to Authority
**Description**: Accepting a claim solely because an authority figure endorses it.
**Research Example**: "It was shown at the Venice Biennale, so the work's claims must be sound."
**Correct Approach**: Even prestigious venues exhibit work whose stated claims don't hold up. Evaluate on merit.

### 7. Appeal to Tradition
**Description**: Arguing something is correct because it has always been done that way.
**Research Example**: "This medium has been the standard for 30 years, so it must be the right choice for the work."
**Correct Approach**: Evaluate whether the medium actually serves this work's concept.

### 8. Appeal to Novelty
**Description**: Arguing something is better because it's new.
**Research Example**: "This work uses the newest AI model, so it must be more significant than earlier pieces."
**Correct Approach**: Novelty of tools doesn't equal artistic significance. Argue on the work.

### 9. Appeal to Popularity (Bandwagon)
**Description**: Arguing something is true because many people believe it.
**Research Example**: "Most media artists now use this toolkit, so it must be the best for my work."
**Correct Approach**: Popularity doesn't validate a choice. Assess fit to the concept independently.

### 10. Red Herring
**Description**: Introducing an irrelevant topic to divert from the argument.
**Research Example**: Responding to criticism of an under-anchored reception claim by discussing how urgent and important the work's theme is.

### Evidence Fallacies

### 11. Cherry-Picking (Selection Bias)
**Description**: Selecting evidence that supports the conclusion while ignoring contradictory evidence.
**Research Example**: Citing 5 precedent works that flatter the claimed novelty while omitting 12 closer precedents that undercut it.
**Detection**: Compare cited precedents against a comprehensive lineage search.

### 12. Confirmation Bias
**Description**: Seeking, interpreting, and remembering information that confirms pre-existing beliefs.
**Research Example**: Reading only audience comments that confirm the work "moved" people, discounting indifferent or hostile responses.
**Detection**: Check if reception was sampled neutrally; look for actively sought disconfirming responses.

### 13. Survivorship Bias
**Description**: Drawing conclusions only from "survivors" (successes), ignoring those that didn't survive.
**Research Example**: "Every celebrated AI artwork used technique X" — ignoring the many works that used X and were forgotten.
**Detection**: Ask "what about the works that used the same approach and failed?"

### 14. Anecdotal Evidence
**Description**: Using individual stories as proof of a general claim.
**Research Example**: "One visitor wept at the installation, so the work reliably produces catharsis."
**Detection**: Is this a recurring observed pattern or an isolated anecdote?

### 15. Hasty Generalization
**Description**: Drawing broad conclusions from insufficient evidence.
**Research Example**: "Three visitors at one opening reacted this way, therefore all audiences experience the work this way."
**Detection**: Is the observation representative? Is the generalization proportionate to the evidence? (See the evidence model's caution against over-claiming generalizability.)

### Causal Fallacies

### 16. Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc
**Description**: Assuming that because B followed A, A caused B.
**Research Example**: "After we added the interactive layer, attendance rose. Therefore the interactivity caused the rise."
**Detection**: Were there confounders (a press feature, a festival slot)? Any comparison condition?

### 17. Cum Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc (Correlation ≠ Causation)
**Description**: Assuming that correlation implies causation.
**Research Example**: "Works shown at major festivals get more scholarly attention, so festival selection causes scholarly attention."
**Detection**: Is there a plausible mechanism? Could both be driven by a third factor (the work's significance)?

### 18. Reverse Causation
**Description**: Getting cause and effect backwards.
**Research Example**: "Critical acclaim makes a work feel innovative" when actually the work's innovation drew the acclaim.
**Detection**: Consider temporal order and alternative causal directions.

### 19. Ecological Fallacy
**Description**: Inferring individual-level conclusions from group-level data.
**Research Example**: "Cities with more public art have higher wellbeing scores, so seeing public art makes a given individual happier."
**Detection**: Are individual-level and group-level relationships the same?

### 20. Simpson's Paradox
**Description**: A trend present in subgroups reverses when groups are combined.
**Research Example**: Two exhibition rooms each show rising average dwell time, but the show overall shows falling dwell time (because visitor mix shifted between rooms).
**Detection**: Always check disaggregated data alongside aggregate.

### Reasoning Fallacies

### 21. Straw Man
**Description**: Misrepresenting an opponent's argument to make it easier to attack.
**Research Example**: Reviewer says "the reception claim is under-anchored" → Artist responds "my reviewer says the whole work is worthless."
**Detection**: Does the refutation address the actual criticism?

### 22. Moving the Goalposts
**Description**: Changing the criteria for success after seeing results.
**Research Example**: Defining the work's success as "provoking debate," then shifting to "aesthetic beauty" when the debate doesn't materialize.
**Detection**: Were the work's stated aims fixed before claiming success?

### 23. Slippery Slope
**Description**: Arguing that one action will inevitably lead to an extreme outcome.
**Research Example**: "If we let AI generate part of the image, authorship will dissolve entirely and art will lose all meaning."
**Detection**: Is each step in the chain actually probable?

### 24. Circular Reasoning (Begging the Question)
**Description**: The conclusion is assumed in the premise.
**Research Example**: "This work is important because it is influential, and it is influential because it is important."
**Detection**: Does the argument depend on the truth of what it's trying to prove?

### 25. No True Scotsman
**Description**: Redefining a category to exclude counterexamples.
**Research Example**: "All generative art is unpredictable." "But work X is fully deterministic." "Well, X isn't truly generative art."
**Detection**: Is the definition being modified to fit the claim?

### 26. Equivocation
**Description**: Using a term in two different senses within the same argument.
**Research Example**: "Autonomous" used sometimes to mean "runs without input" and sometimes to mean "makes its own creative decisions."
**Detection**: Is the key term defined consistently throughout? (See glossary §3 on generative/interactive/autonomous.)

### Statistical Fallacies

### 27. Base Rate Neglect
**Description**: Ignoring the base rate (overall probability) in favor of specific information.
**Research Example**: "90% of visitors stayed past five minutes" — but the gallery's baseline dwell rate for any room is already 88%.
**Detection**: Always compare against relevant base rates.

### 28. Regression to the Mean
**Description**: Extreme performances naturally tend back toward average on subsequent measurements.
**Research Example**: "After the workshop, the least-engaged visitors engaged more" — the most extreme cases tend to move toward the mean regardless.
**Detection**: Was there a comparison condition? Were initial measurements extreme?

### 29. Texas Sharpshooter
**Description**: Finding a pattern in random data by focusing on clusters and ignoring misses.
**Research Example**: Generating 200 outputs from the model and presenting only the 3 that look intentional, framing them as the system's characteristic behaviour.
**Detection**: Was the selection criterion stated? Is the curation disclosed as curation?

### 30. Gambler's Fallacy
**Description**: Believing past random events influence future random events.
**Research Example**: "The series has felt stale for five iterations, so the next run is bound to surprise."
**Detection**: Is there a causal mechanism for change, or is this just pattern-seeking?

### 31. McNamara Fallacy (Quantitative Bias)
**Description**: Making decisions based solely on quantitative metrics while ignoring qualitative factors.
**Research Example**: Judging the work's success only by visitor-count and dwell-time metrics, ignoring critical depth and conceptual impact.
**Detection**: Are important but hard-to-measure factors being excluded?

### 32. Goodhart's Law
**Description**: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."
**Research Example**: Optimizing a work for "Instagrammability" (shares as the target) instead of the artistic concept.
**Detection**: Has the metric become a target? Are there signs of metric manipulation?

## Quick Reference: Detection Questions

| Ask This | Detects |
|----------|---------|
| "Does B have other possible causes?" | Post hoc, false cause |
| "What about the failures?" | Survivorship bias |
| "Is this sample representative?" | Hasty generalization |
| "Were criteria defined before results?" | Moving goalposts, Texas sharpshooter |
| "Is the key term used consistently?" | Equivocation |
| "What's the base rate?" | Base rate neglect |
| "What evidence was left out?" | Cherry-picking, confirmation bias |
| "Is this the actual argument being made?" | Straw man |
| "Can we distinguish correlation from causation?" | Cum hoc, ecological fallacy |
| "Are individual and group levels being mixed?" | Ecological fallacy, Simpson's paradox |
